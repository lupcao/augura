Ogham reading

Draw one letter, or three, from the 20 feda of the early Irish tree alphabet. This page has a free browser tool at the top and a guide to the Ogham mode in the Augura iPhone app (coming soon to the App Store). The browser tool is simpler than the app mode: there is no web version of the full mode.

What it is

Ogham is an early Irish alphabet of straight strokes cut along the edge of a standing stone. Its 20 main letters are called feda, and each one is named for a tree or plant: Beith (birch), Dair (oak), Idad (yew) and so on. Augura draws the letter on a vertical stave, with the strokes marked against a long stem, and gives you a short reading.

How to use it

  • Spread: one stave, or three staves for past, present and future. The three are always different.
  • Stave look (in the settings) chooses the surface: Standing stone, Hazel wand, Ink on vellum or Gold on black.

Focus on a question, then tap Draw or shake your phone.

The 20 letters and their trees

Aicme Beithe

Strokes to one side of the stem, 1 to 5

Beith (birch), Luis (rowan), Fearn (alder), Sail (willow), Nion (ash)

Aicme Úatha

Strokes to the other side of the stem, 1 to 5

Úath (hawthorn), Dair (oak), Tinne (holly), Coll (hazel), Ceirt (apple)

Aicme Muine

Slanting strokes across the stem, 1 to 5

Muin (vine), Gort (ivy), Ngéadal (reed), Straif (blackthorn), Ruis (elder)

Aicme Ailme

Short notches through the stem, 1 to 5 (the vowels)

Ailm (silver fir), Onn (gorse), Úr (heather), Edad (aspen), Idad (yew)

How to read it

Start with the tree: picture it, its season, how it grows. The reading grows from that image. Open Lore and kennings to see facts about the tree and, where we know them, the medieval phrases for the letter. In a three-stave spread, read the letters together as one short story.

The odds

Each letter has a 1 in 20 chance. A three-stave spread has 6,840 possible ordered outcomes.

Where it comes from

About 400 ogham stones survive, mostly in southern Ireland and western Britain, carved between roughly the 4th and 7th centuries CE; most say little more than a name. The tree names come from medieval manuscripts, especially the Auraicept na n-Éces ("The Scholars' Primer"), which also lists word-oghams: short poetic phrases for each letter, such as "fairest of trees" for Coll (hazel) or "oldest of woods" for Idad (yew).

The letters fall into four groups of five, called aicmí: strokes on one side of the stem, strokes on the other side, slanting strokes across it, and short notches for the vowels. There is no evidence that the early Irish used ogham for divination. The popular "tree calendar" of Robert Graves is a twentieth-century invention, so Augura does not use it. The meanings here are our own, drawn from the trees themselves and the old word-oghams.

What it cannot do

  • It is not an ogham alphabet translator: it draws a letter and reads it, and does not convert your words into ogham.
  • It does not use the popular tree calendar of Robert Graves, which is a twentieth-century invention.
  • The browser tool has no stave looks (stone, wand, vellum), no shake, history or share card: those are in the app. It saves nothing.
  • It does not predict anything. The meanings are Augura's own, drawn from the trees and the old word-oghams, for reflection.
